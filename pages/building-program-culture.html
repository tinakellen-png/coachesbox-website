Building a Program Players Want to Be Part Of

Culture is not a poster on the wall. It is what your players do when you are not watching.

The Coaches' Box · June 2026 · 6 min read

Early in my career I thought culture was something you talked about. I gave speeches, I hung slogans in the locker room, and I told my teams what we stood for. Then I watched what actually happened when things got hard, and I realized the truth. Culture is not what you say. It is what you tolerate, what you reward, and what you model every single day. Players learn your real values by watching your behavior, not by reading your signs.

The good news is that this means culture is something you can build on purpose. It is not luck and it is not about having the most talent. Some of the strongest cultures I have ever seen were on teams that were undermanned and outsized. Here is how I approach building a program players are proud to belong to.

Decide What You Stand For, Then Live It

You cannot build a culture around values you do not actually enforce. So I keep it simple. I pick a small number of standards that matter most, I define exactly what they look like in action, and then I hold everyone to them, starting with myself and my staff. If effort is a standard, then I praise effort loudly and I address the lack of it immediately. Players are always watching to see whether the standard is real. The moment you let it slide for your best player, it is no longer a standard.

Coach the Person First

I learned that if I take care of the young person, the player takes care of himself. When a kid knows you care about his life beyond the field, he will run through a wall for you on it. That means learning about their families, checking in on their grades, and noticing when something is off. This is not soft coaching. It is the foundation of trust, and trust is what lets you push players harder than they thought they could go.

Let Players Own It

A culture that depends entirely on the head coach is fragile. The strongest programs are the ones where the players hold each other accountable. I build that on purpose by giving leaders real responsibility and letting them set standards in the locker room. When a veteran corrects a younger player before I ever have to, I know the culture is taking root. My goal is to work myself out of the accountability business by handing it to the team.

Make Standards Visible and Repeatable

Culture lives in habits and routines, not big moments. How we warm up, how we finish drills, how we respond to a mistake, how we treat officials and opponents. I make our standards visible in the daily details so they become automatic. When the routine is strong, players do the right thing under pressure because it is simply what they always do.

Celebrate the Right Things

Players repeat what gets rewarded. If I only celebrate touchdowns, I teach them that only the scoreboard matters. So I make a point of celebrating the hidden things that win over time. The perfect assignment. The extra film session. The teammate who lifts someone up. When you shine a light on the behaviors you want, you get more of them.

The Bottom Line

You build a program players love the same way you build anything that lasts, one honest day at a time. Decide what you stand for and live it. Care about the person first. Give players ownership. Turn your standards into daily habits. Celebrate the things that truly matter. Do that consistently and you will not have to sell your players on the culture. They will build it right alongside you, and they will carry it long after they leave your program.

Build the culture. Sharpen the reps.

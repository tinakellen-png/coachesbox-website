Why Mental Reps Stick, and How to Run Walkthroughs That Actually Work

The reps you cannot see are often the ones that win on Friday.

The Coaches' Box · June 2026 · 6 min read

When people picture a great practice, they picture full speed, pads popping, and bodies flying around. I love that energy as much as anyone. But some of the most important learning my players ever did happened at a walking pace, with no contact at all. Mental reps and walkthroughs are the quiet engine of a well prepared team, and most coaches leave that engine idling.

Here is what I have come to believe after years on the field. A player does not truly know an assignment until they can see it in their mind before it happens. If they have to react and think at the same time, they are already late. Mental reps train the seeing. Once a player can picture the play clearly, their body follows without hesitation. Let me walk you through how I use this.

What a Mental Rep Really Is

A mental rep is a full quality repetition that happens in the mind instead of the body. It is not daydreaming and it is not standing around. When I put a player in a mental rep, I want them locked in on their exact assignment, seeing the formation, hearing the call, and feeling the movement. Done right, the brain barely knows the difference between a vivid mental rep and a physical one. That is why players who visualize well perform better under pressure. They have already been there.

Coach the Line, Not Just the Field

The group waiting for their turn is a coaching opportunity, not a rest break. I station a coach or a trusted player with the waiting group and give them a job on every snap. Watch your key. Call out your assignment. Mirror the footwork. When the next group steps up, they have already run the play three times in their head. This is how you get one hundred reps out of a period that only allows thirty live ones.

How to Run a Walkthrough That Teaches

A walkthrough is not just a slow version of practice. It is a teaching tool with its own rules. Here is how I keep them sharp:

  • Move at teaching speed, not sleepwalking speed. Slow enough to fix details, fast enough to hold attention.
  • Stop and correct the moment something is wrong, then run it again correctly so the last rep is a good one.
  • Talk through assignments out loud. When players say their job while they do it, it locks in deeper.
  • Keep everyone engaged. No one stands with their helmet off watching. If they are not repping, they are mental repping.
  • Finish on a clean rep. The brain remembers the last thing it did, so never end on a mistake.

Use the Walkthrough Before the Install

I like to walk through a new concept before we ever run it live. When players have seen it slow and correct, they run it live with far fewer mistakes. It feels slower in the moment, but it saves us from burning live reps on confusion. Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast.

Bring the Classroom to the Field

Everything I just described works even better when players can actually see the play the way they will experience it. Whether you use a screen, a board, or just disciplined walkthroughs, the principle is the same. Help players see it first, and the doing takes care of itself.

The Bottom Line

You do not need more bodies or more field time to teach better. You need to treat the reps players take in their minds as seriously as the ones they take with their pads. Coach the sideline, run walkthroughs with purpose, and help every player see the play before it happens. Do that, and you will be shocked how much sharper your team looks when the lights come on.
